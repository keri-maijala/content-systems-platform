# Voice and Tone — Default

This document defines the default voice and tone for the Content Systems Platform. It ships with every client instance and is replaced in full by client-specific voice and tone developed during a consulting engagement.

---

## Voice

Voice is consistent across the entire experience. It reflects who we are, regardless of what we're asking the user to do or how they're feeling in the moment.

### Core characteristics

**Conversational but professional**
Write the way a knowledgeable colleague speaks — not like a legal document, and not like a text message. Use plain language, natural contractions, and real sentences. Avoid jargon, buzzwords, and corporate filler.

**Helpful without being condescending**
Assume users are capable. Offer guidance when it's needed, not as a default. Never over-explain something a reasonable person would already know. If something is complex, explain it clearly — don't apologize for it or talk around it.

**Concise without being abrupt**
Say what needs to be said and stop. Don't pad, repeat, or add filler for the sake of warmth. But don't strip content so bare that it feels cold or incomplete. Every word should earn its place.

**Encouraging without being overbearing**
Acknowledge progress and effort where it's genuine. Don't cheer for routine actions or congratulate users for things that aren't accomplishments. Encouragement should feel like it means something.

**Never snarky or too casual**
Humor, when used, should be warm and inclusive — never at the user's expense, never edgy, never forced. When in doubt, leave it out.

---

## Tone

Tone shifts across the experience to match the user's emotional context. The characteristics above remain constant; what changes is how they're expressed — the warmth, urgency, formality, and emotional weight of the content.

Each stage below identifies the user's likely emotional state and the tone that best serves them in that moment.

---

### Marketing site

**User's emotional state:** Curious, evaluating, possibly skeptical. They're forming a first impression and deciding whether to trust the product enough to engage further.

**Tone:** Confident and clear. Lead with value, not features. Don't oversell or use superlatives — they erode trust. Be direct about what the product does and who it's for. Invite without pressuring.

**Watch out for:** Hype, vague promises, exclamation points, and language that overstates what the product delivers.

---

### Sign-up

**User's emotional state:** Interested but cautious. They've decided to try, but they're committing something — an email address, personal information, maybe payment. There's low-level friction and mild vulnerability.

**Tone:** Reassuring and efficient. Respect their time and their data. Be transparent about what you're asking for and why. Don't celebrate prematurely — they haven't gotten value yet. Keep it moving.

**Watch out for:** Over-explaining, false urgency, premature enthusiasm, and burying important information (privacy, terms) in fine print.

---

### Onboarding and follow-up

**User's emotional state:** Hopeful but uncertain. They want to succeed and may feel some pressure to "get it right." First impressions of the product experience are forming.

**Tone:** Warm, patient, and orienting. Help them feel competent quickly. Celebrate real milestones — first meaningful action, setup complete — but don't celebrate every click. Follow-up communications should feel like a helpful nudge, not a drip campaign.

**Watch out for:** Information overload, fake enthusiasm, condescension disguised as helpfulness, and follow-up emails that feel automated or impersonal.

---

### Core tasks

**User's emotional state:** Focused and goal-oriented. They know what they're trying to do. They don't want friction or interruption.

**Tone:** Efficient and clear. Get out of the way. Labels, instructions, and confirmations should be precise and predictable. Don't editorialize. Let the work speak.

**Watch out for:** Unnecessary copy, clever labels that sacrifice clarity, and microcopy that interrupts the flow with personality when the user just wants to complete a task.

---

### Errors

**User's emotional state:** Frustrated, confused, or anxious. Something didn't work. They may feel like they did something wrong, even if they didn't.

**Tone:** Calm, clear, and constructive. Acknowledge what happened without blame. Tell them what to do next. If it's our fault, own it plainly — don't hide behind passive voice or vague language. If it's recoverable, make that clear immediately.

**Watch out for:** Blame ("You entered an invalid..."), technical jargon, vague error messages that don't help ("Something went wrong"), and false positivity that minimizes a real problem.

---

### Empty states

**User's emotional state:** Variable. Could be new (haven't started yet), stuck (don't know what to do), or returning after a gap. The emotional stakes are low, but the opportunity is high.

**Tone:** Inviting and action-oriented. An empty state is an opportunity, not an absence. Help the user see what's possible and give them a clear first step. Don't dwell on the emptiness — point toward the value.

**Watch out for:** Cute copy that prioritizes personality over utility, and empty states that just say nothing is here without telling the user what to do.

---

### Notifications

**User's emotional state:** Interrupted. Notifications arrive in the middle of something else. Attention is a cost.

**Tone:** Direct and purposeful. Every notification should earn its place. Lead with the most important information. Be specific about what happened and what (if anything) the user needs to do. Match urgency to actual urgency — don't cry wolf.

**Watch out for:** Vague subject lines, unnecessary notifications that erode trust, urgency that isn't real, and notifications that don't tell the user what action to take.

---

### Offboarding

**User's emotional state:** Variable and often significant. They may be frustrated or disappointed with the product. They may simply be done — moving on without strong feelings. Or they may be leaving because something in their life has resolved — a difficult situation they no longer need help with — and feel relief or even genuine happiness about closing this chapter. The emotional stakes of offboarding are easy to underestimate.

**Tone:** Respectful, frictionless, and attuned. Don't guilt, don't beg, don't hide the exit. Acknowledge their decision without projecting how they must be feeling. Make the process clear and fast. If there's something they should know before they go (data export, account implications), say it plainly and early — not as a barrier, but as a service. Let them leave on their own terms.

**Watch out for:** Dark patterns, manipulative language ("Are you sure? You'll lose everything"), excessive steps, copy that assumes the user is unhappy, and copy that assumes the user is happy. Both are wrong half the time.

---

### Trust and safety moments

**User's emotional state:** Alert, possibly anxious or on guard. These are moments where something important is at stake — security, privacy, sensitive data, or a significant decision.

**Tone:** Serious, clear, and direct. This is not the place for warmth or wit. Be precise about what is happening, what it means, and what the user's options are. Don't minimize risk. Don't alarm unnecessarily. Give the user control.

**Watch out for:** Legalese, euphemism, alarm language that causes panic without helping, and copy that buries critical information in reassuring-sounding filler.

---

### Transactional

**User's emotional state:** Task-focused and expecting reliability. Receipts, confirmations, invoices, and summaries need to be accurate and easy to parse — not an opportunity for personality.

**Tone:** Precise, neutral, and complete. Include everything the user needs. Use clear labels and consistent formatting. A transactional message that requires interpretation has failed.

**Watch out for:** Copy that tries too hard to be friendly in a context that calls for accuracy, missing information, and inconsistent terminology that causes confusion.

|---|---|---|

| Core tasks | Focused, goal-oriented | Efficient, clear, unobtrusive |
| Errors | Frustrated, anxious | Calm, constructive, owning |
